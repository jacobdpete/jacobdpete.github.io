The plains had always been empty. They'd been empty in 1005 when Roqus the Terrible burned the forests in the west. They'd been empty in 1657 when Captain Rouzet first met the tribes of the expanse, the plane on which all existed and all ended. They'd been empty in 1931 when industrialization spread its tangle of railroads across the continent, gorging itself on the bison that roamed the plains, the corn that grew in its soil, and the men that walked the grasses.

And the plains remained empty in what was probably March of 1990, when men in their bunkers had launched the nukes and killed the class traitors and patriots and the homosexuals and the black man and the brown man and the white man.

The wind found its way through a break in the hills, through a lonesome tree, and through the wheel wells of a rusted Navarro Motors H-600. The boy steps off his motorbike. The dinky old thing looks pristine next to the 600. Purring, it glimmers in the mid-day sun and heats an ass made cold by the plains in march.

He's a mailman. Or dressed like one. In a post office nine kilometers west of Fort Lenox, Bria. Fort Lenox obeyed no king.
