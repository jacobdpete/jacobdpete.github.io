The plains had always been empty. They'd been empty in 1005 when Roqus the Terrible burned the forests in the west. They'd been empty in 1657 when Captain Rouzet first met the tribes of the expanse, the plane on which all existed and all ended. They'd been empty in 1931 when industrialization spread its tangle of railroads across the continent, gorging itself on the bison that roamed the plains, the corn that grew in its soil, and the men that walked the grasses.

And the plains remained empty in what was probably March of 1990, when men in their bunkers had launched the nukes and killed the class traitors and the patriots and the minorities and the majorities.

The wind found its way through a break in the hills, through a lonesome tree, and through the wheel wells of a rusted Navarro Motors H-600. The boy steps off his motorbike. The dinky old thing looks pristine next to the 600. Purring, it glimmers in the mid-day sun and heats an ass made cold by the plains in march.

He's a mailman. Or dressed like one. In a post office nine kilometers west of Fort Lenox, Bria. Fort Lenox obeyed no king, so it was easy to swipe gas as long as you weren't an idiot about it. Dominion, twenty kilometers north, was bigger, but there were far more bodies clamoring for warmth there. The boy had no interest in going back.

The boy turns the motorbike off and attempts to open the door. Locked. He shimmies through the back window and crawls into the driver's seat, pops the hood, and crawls back out the way he came. It's a methodical movement and he'd done the same thing to dozens of pick-ups on the highway in Dominion. Back at the front of the car, he carefully raises the hood until he can inspect the guts of the truck. He curses his luck and shuts the hood. Nothing worth swiping for the bike. 

Now sure the battery is dead, he smashes in the window of the car with a can and throws himself inside. He lays there across the bench seat, breathing the stale, bitter air through a dry throat and staring at the rusted aluminum roof through crusted eyes. He exhales a gravely sigh and turns over, aching for sleep, but the polyester mailman shirt itches like hell and he can't get comfortable.

The boy gives up and grasps under the dashboard until he finds the glovebox latch. He yanks it until the box swings down, revealing eight tissues, three packets of hot sauce, and a pistol. 

The pistol brings him home to his grandfather's shed, the one with the assault rifles and grenades and plate armor from when he fought in the Helsayan uprising. The first time he shot a gun was at a range, with his uncle swearing in his ear to steady his hands. His uncle flinched when he rested his hands on the trigger and took the gun from the boy as soon as he'd fired his first shot.

The second time he shot a gun was in Dominion, when a short man had tried to grab canned green beans from his bag at an empty supermarket. It was a week after the nukes and natural selection had yet to pick off people like the man. With Darwin on his mind and nothing else, the boy pulled a pistol from his pocket and shot the man in the stomach.

And the boy let the wind take him from Dominion to Fort Lenox, and he hadn't seen a soul since.
