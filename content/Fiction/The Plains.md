The plains had always been empty. They'd been empty in 1005 when Roqus the Terrible burned the forests in the west. They'd been empty in 1657 when Captain Rouzet first met the tribes of the expanse, the plane on which all existed and all ended. They'd been empty in 1931 when industrialization spread its tangle of railroads across the continent, gorging itself on the bison that roamed the plains, the corn that grew in its soil, and the men that walked the grasses.

And the plains remained empty in what was probably March of 1990, when men in their bunkers had launched the nukes and killed the class traitors and the patriots and the minorities and the majorities.

The wind found its way through a break in the hills, through a lonesome tree, and through the wheel wells of a rusted Navarro Motors H-600. The boy steps off his motorbike. The dinky old thing looks pristine next to the 600. Purring, it glimmers in the mid-day sun and heats an ass made cold by the plains in march.

He's a mailman. Or dressed like one. In a post office nine kilometers west of Fort Lenox, Bria. Fort Lenox obeyed no king, so it was easy to swipe gas as long as you weren't an idiot about it. Dominion, twenty kilometers north, was bigger, but there were far more bodies clamoring for warmth there. The boy had no interest in going back.

The boy turns the motorbike off and attempts to open the door. Locked. He shimmies through the back window and crawls into the driver's seat, pops the hood, and crawls back out the way he came. It's a methodical movement and he'd done the same thing to dozens of pick-ups on the highway in Dominion. Back at the front of the car, he carefully raises the hood until he can inspect the guts of the truck. He curses his luck and shuts the hood. Nothing worth swiping for the bike. 

Now sure the battery is dead, he smashes in the window of the car with a can and throws himself inside. He lays there across the bench seat, breathing the stale, bitter air through a dry throat and staring at the rusted aluminum roof through crusted eyes. He exhales a gravelly sigh and turns over, aching for sleep, but the polyester mailman shirt itches like hell and he can't get comfortable.

The boy gives up and grasps under the dashboard until he finds the glovebox latch. He yanks it until the box swings down, revealing eight tissues, three packets of hot sauce, and a pistol. 

The pistol brings him home to his grandfather's shed, the one with the assault rifles and grenades and plate armor from the Helsayan uprising. The first time he shot a gun was at a range, with his uncle swearing in his ear to steady his hands. His uncle flinched when he rested his finger on the trigger and took the gun from the boy as soon as he'd fired his first shot.

The second time he shot a gun was in Dominion, when a short man had tried to grab canned green beans from the boy's bag at an empty supermarket. It was a week after the nukes and natural selection had yet to pick off people like the man. With Darwin on his mind and nothing else, the boy pulled a pistol from his pocket and shot the man in the stomach.

And the boy let the wind take him from Dominion to Fort Lenox, and he hadn't seen a soul since.

Fort Lenox was a sleepless misadventure for the boy. The migraines and vomit crippled him for weeks until his body reverted to its drive for survival, and the second gunshot was expunged. Paranoid he would meet another thief, he left Lenox after a couple months and wandered the plains in between, sleeping in empty homes.

This was when the boy began to collect pictures. Outside Fort Lenox was a homestead with albums and albums of family photos and amateur photography. His uncle - the other one - had taken up photography, but the boy's family rarely kept photos.

The photos could open worlds to the boy he couldn't imagine. Splayed out on the floor of a post office, a gas station, a truck bed, he'd hold the photos up to block the sun and fall into a reality of affluent young women or blue-collar men. Being alone was easy, but loneliness was a whole different thing.

The boy pockets the pistol, tissues, and hot sauce and mounts the motorbike. The sun is beginning to go down as he motors through a neglected wheat field, seeing nothing but yellow until the blocky shape of his home - for now - moves into view. With the sun gone, he fumbles in a pocket for the garage remote. He briefly thanks Demei it hasn't died yet, because there'd be no way to get batteries for it without going back to Dominion or trading with someone else out in the plains. 

His house, not yet a home, is a small farmhouse, probably owned by Helsayan sharecroppers. With little already there, he can, crow-like, fill it with whatever he takes home on the motorbike. Parking, he enters the home, scarfs down an MRE, and goes to sleep.

He dreams of the plains burning under Roqus. He dreams of Rouzet massacring the tribes. He dreams of a factory on a hill of dry grass, building bombs. He dreams of the lights of December 1989, the world's worst fireworks show, and he wonders how many are left? How many wander the plains like him? How many have taken the life of another wanderer, and fled like a coward? How many wanderers will no longer go to Dominion? 

Like clockwork, he wakes up, pushes an MRE down his throat, scavenges. On and on and on until the polyester has worn through and he is no longer a mail man. He is an animal prowling the plains, a vulture circling a corpse, a vampire suckling the blood of the plains.

Searching for his last meal, the boy goes back to Dominion.
